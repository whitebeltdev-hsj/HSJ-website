First-Timer Basics

What to Expect at Your First Class

The honest walkthrough of your first hour on the mats, from the front desk to the drive home.

Jay Shellhammer

Black Belt · Co-Owner

Before you walk in

Come 10 to 15 minutes before class starts. That gives us time to say hello, show you around, and get you signed in without rushing. Wear shorts and a t-shirt. If you want to try the traditional uniform, we'll lend you a gi, so there is nothing to buy for your first visit.

Bring a water bottle and a pair of sandals. You train barefoot on the mat, and the sandals are for everywhere else. That is genuinely the whole packing list.

The part everyone worries about

Almost everyone walks in a little unsure. New room, new people, a sport you have only seen on a screen. Here is the honest version: the nerves fade fast, usually before the warm up ends. Your first class is about learning the basics and feeling out the room, nothing more.

There is no live sparring until you decide you are ready. Nobody throws the new person to the wolves here. You can even watch a class first if that feels better. Come in, take a seat, and see the room for yourself. Plenty of people do exactly that.

What the hour actually looks like

Class starts with a warm up. Expect light movement, some stretching, and drills that get you used to moving on the mats. Then the coach teaches a technique, broken into steps, and you drill it with a partner at a pace you control. If you are brand new, your entry point is the Fundamentals class, built for people with zero experience.

Your partner knows it is your first day. The people you will train beside remember their own first class, and the camaraderie in this room is the thing visitors mention most. Ask questions. Go slow. Nobody is grading you.

Who you will meet

The room is a genuine mix: parents and kids under one roof, first-timers finding their feet, and serious competitors sharpening for their next match. Kids ages 4 to 15 have their own classes, and plenty of parents train while their children do, or watch from the benches. The front desk knows every face, and on your first night a coach will make a point of learning yours.

That mix is deliberate. The same coaches who develop competitors teach a six-year-old to tie a belt, and the standard is the same at every level: patient, technical, and welcoming. You are not joining a class. You are joining a room that already knows how to take care of new people.

After class

You will be tired, probably a little sore tomorrow, and most likely surprised by how fast the hour went. A coach will answer your questions, and then you decide in your own time. The price is on the wall and on the pricing page, and there is no hard sell, ever.
